# 01 · Newton's orbit waited 150 years for a proof

- **Length:** about 80 seconds.
- **Hook:** Newton's orbit rests on one sentence that nobody proved for a
  century and a half, and the proof, once written, was withheld.

## Narration and scenes

| Time | Visual | On-screen text | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0–5 s | A polygon of straight steps around a sun, one kick at each corner | *Principia*, 1687 | In 1687 Newton proved Kepler's area law with a polygon. |
| 5–18 s | Equal time slices tick by; each straight step is followed by a kick toward the sun | "Dividatur tempus in partes æquales" | Divide time into equal parts. In each part the body moves straight. At every corner the sun gives it one kick. |
| 18–30 s | The polygon refines, its corners multiply and smooth into an ellipse | "… in infinitum … erit linea curva" | Then one sentence: increase the number of triangles, shrink their width without end, and the perimeter becomes a curve. |
| 30–38 s | The ellipse freezes; a question mark hovers over it | Does the polygon settle on one curve? | That sentence carries the whole argument. For well over a century, nobody proved it. |
| 38–52 s | Title page of Moigno's 1844 *Leçons*, then a highlighted passage | "imprimée … pas été livrées au public" | The proof came from Augustin Cauchy. In 1844 the abbé Moigno, publishing Cauchy's methods, wrote that it had been printed, but the sheets were never released to the public, so it was very little known. |
| 52–68 s | A coarse polygon and a finer one, overlaid; the gap between them fades | The subdivision stops mattering | Cauchy's argument is Newton's polygon made honest. Compare a coarse division with a finer one, show that the difference fades, and the limit depends only on the starting point and the law of motion, provided the law is smooth enough. |
| 68–80 s | Peano's 1890 page with its first footnote highlighted | Peano, 1890: "donnée par Cauchy" | In 1890 Giuseppe Peano credited Cauchy, and called that publication incomplete. Newton's curve got its proof about a century and a half late, from a lecture almost nobody saw. |

## Sources and status

- **VERIFIED** Proposition I's division of time and its limiting sentence:
  Principia 1687, NATP00077.par45 ("Dividatur tempus in partes æquales";
  "Augeatur jam numerus & minuatur latitudo triangulorum in infinitum, &
  eorum ultima perimeter ADF … erit linea curva").
- **VERIFIED** Moigno, *Leçons de calcul différentiel et de calcul intégral*,
  tome 2 (Paris, 1844), introduction p. xxxv: the method "a été imprimée,
  mais les feuilles qui la contenaient n'ont pas été livrées au public; elle
  est par conséquent très-peu connue."
- **VERIFIED** Cauchy's partition independence and existence statement:
  Moigno 1844, pp. 394 and 396; quoted in
  [EXISTENCE_OF_THE_CURVE](../../research/EXISTENCE_OF_THE_CURVE.md).
- **VERIFIED** Peano, *Mathematische Annalen* 37 (1890), p. 182, note: the
  proof "a été donnée par Cauchy, et (incomplétement) publiée par Moigno".
- **SECONDARY** Cauchy taught the method at the École polytechnique around
  1824 (Gilain's 1981 edition of the course, as reported in secondary
  sources). The script avoids the date.
- **SECONDARY** "For well over a century, nobody proved it": the standard
  account credits Cauchy with the first rigorous existence proof for
  differential equations. Peano's note supports Cauchy's priority.

## Do not say

- That Cauchy hid the proof. Moigno reports that the printed sheets were not
  released; he gives no reason.
- That Newton was wrong. Cauchy's theorem confirms the curve under a
  smoothness condition that holds for gravity away from the sun.
- "157 years" without saying it counts from 1687 to Moigno's 1844 book.
