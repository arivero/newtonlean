# 02 · The line Newton never proved

- **Length:** about 85 seconds.
- **Hook:** In 1713 Newton added one sentence to the *Principia* that
  classical physics has leaned on ever since. He gave no proof.

## Narration and scenes

| Time | Visual | On-screen text | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0–6 s | A ball launched from a point in several directions, each path bending into a conic | Book I, Proposition XIII | Throw a body from any point, at any speed, under an inverse-square force, and Newton says it moves on a conic. |
| 6–14 s | The 1687 page, the corollary in one line | 1687: the claim alone | In 1687 that is all he says. |
| 14–28 s | Two curves drawn touching at one point, then a red cross between them | "Orbes duo se mutuo tangentes, eadem vi centripeta describi non possunt" | In 1713 he adds a reason: two orbits that touch each other cannot be traced by the same centripetal force. |
| 28–38 s | The phrase "eademque velocitate" slides into the sentence | 1726: "… eademque velocitate" | In 1726 he repairs it: the same force and the same velocity. |
| 38–48 s | Three dials, position, velocity, force, locking a single path | Position + velocity + force = one path? | That clause is a big claim. Give me the position, the velocity and the force, and the path is fixed. Newton states it and moves on. |
| 48–56 s | The inverse-square field around a sun, a single path highlighted | True for smooth forces | For gravity away from the sun it is true. Cauchy and Lipschitz proved it later, for forces smooth enough. |
| 56–72 s | Graph: a curve sitting on zero, then peeling off at t = 1, t = 2, t = 3 | dx/dt = 3x^(2/3) | In 1890 Peano showed what happens otherwise. Starting at zero, this equation has the solution zero, the solution t cubed, and solutions that wait at zero and leave at any moment you choose. |
| 72–85 s | A ball resting on the top of a dome, then rolling off at an unpredictable moment | Norton's dome | The philosopher John Norton built a mechanical version: a ball at rest on a specially shaped dome may roll off at any time. Read that way, Newton's one-line clause is a physical assumption, and it fails without smoothness. |

## Sources and status

- **VERIFIED** 1687, NATP00077.par109: Corollary I states the conic claim,
  ending "& contra", with no reason given.
- **VERIFIED** 1713, NATP00082.par140: "Datur autem curvatura ex data vi
  centripeta: & Orbes duo se mutuo tangentes, eadem vi centripeta describi
  non possunt."
- **VERIFIED** 1726, NATP00087.par130: "Datur autem curvatura ex data vi
  centripeta, & velocitate corporis: & orbes duo se mutuo tangentes eadem vi
  centripeta eademque velocitate describi non possunt."
- **VERIFIED** Peano, *Mathematische Annalen* 37 (1890), p. 227: for
  dx/dt = 3x^(2/3) the solutions vanishing with t are x = t³, x = 0, and the
  functions that are zero up to t₁ and equal (t − t₁)³ afterwards.
- **VERIFIED** Peano 1890, pp. 207–208: "On a ainsi démontré les théorèmes
  de Cauchy et de Lipschitz."
- **INTERPRETATION** Reading the 1713 clause as an unproved uniqueness
  postulate; high confidence, recorded in
  [EXISTENCE_OF_THE_CURVE](../../research/EXISTENCE_OF_THE_CURVE.md).
- **MEMORY** Norton's dome: J. D. Norton, "The Dome: An Unexpectedly Simple
  Failure of Determinism", *Philosophy of Science* (2008). Check the
  reference and the dome's shape before production.
- **MEMORY** Johann Bernoulli's objection of about 1710, usually read as the
  prompt for the 1713 sentence. Kept out of the narration.

## Do not say

- That determinism is false. Norton's dome concerns a force that fails a
  smoothness condition; gravity away from the sun satisfies it.
- That Newton's claim about the inverse square is wrong. The uniqueness he
  asserts holds there; he gave no proof of it.
