# 04 · Every derivative subtracts an infinity

- **Length:** about 85 seconds.
- **Hook:** Every derivative you ever took subtracts one infinity from
  another.

## Narration and scenes

| Time | Visual | On-screen text | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0–8 s | The textbook difference quotient written by hand | (f(x+ε) − f(x)) / ε | Take the slope of a curve the textbook way: f of x plus epsilon, minus f of x, all over epsilon. |
| 8–20 s | The fraction splits into two terms; both bars grow without bound as ε shrinks | f(x+ε)/ε − f(x)/ε | Now split the fraction. Each half blows up as epsilon shrinks. The derivative is what survives when you subtract one infinity from the other. |
| 20–30 s | The expansion f(x)/ε + f′(x) + …; the pole term is crossed out | Drop the pole, keep the finite part | Physicists call that move renormalization: expand in epsilon, throw away the infinite part, keep what stays finite. |
| 30–48 s | A falling body leaves its straight inertial path; the small gap, the sagitta, is highlighted | force ∝ sagitta / time² | One way to read Newton's force is the same trick, done twice. He measures force by the departure from straight-line motion, divided by the time squared. Divide the position by the time squared and two infinite terms appear. Inertia, his first law, removes both. What is left over is the force. |
| 48–62 s | Portrait-style card for Berkeley, 1734 | "the Ghosts of departed Quantities" | In 1734 Bishop Berkeley mocked Newton's vanishing increments as the ghosts of departed quantities, and wrote that by a twofold mistake you arrive, though not at science, yet at truth. |
| 62–72 s | Card for Feynman, 1985 | "a dippy process" | Two and a half centuries later Richard Feynman called renormalization a dippy process, and hocus-pocus. |
| 72–85 s | Timeline: Berkeley 1734 → Cauchy 1820s; Feynman 1985 → the algebra of renormalization around 2000 | Same complaint, same trick | Same complaint, same trick. Mathematicians took nearly a century to put the first on solid ground. The second got its algebra around the year 2000. |

## Sources and status

- **VERIFIED** Berkeley, *The Analyst* (1734), §XXXV: "May we not call them
  the Ghosts of departed Quantities?"; §XXII: "But by virtue of a twofold
  mistake you arrive, though not at Science, yet at Truth." Checked against
  the Wilkins edition at maths.tcd.ie.
- **SECONDARY** Feynman, *QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter*
  (1985), p. 128 as cited: "no matter how clever the word, it is what I
  would call a dippy process! Having to resort to such hocus-pocus …".
  Check against the book.
- **VERIFIED** (mathematics) f(x+ε)/ε = f(x)/ε + f′(x) + O(ε); the derivative
  is the finite part after removing the pole.
- **INTERPRETATION** Newton's force as a twice-subtracted finite part with
  inertia as the counterterm: x(t+h)/h² = x(t)/h² + v/h + a/2 + O(h). A
  modern reading of Lemma X and Proposition VI, not Newton's own wording.
- **MEMORY** Connes and Kreimer's algebraic account of renormalization,
  around 1998–2000. Check before naming them on screen.
- **MEMORY** "Nearly a century": Berkeley 1734 to Cauchy's *Cours d'analyse*
  of 1821.

## Do not say

- That Newton did renormalization. The parallel is a modern reading.
- That renormalization is now fully rigorous in general. The algebraic
  account organizes perturbative subtractions; the narration claims only
  that it "got its algebra".
