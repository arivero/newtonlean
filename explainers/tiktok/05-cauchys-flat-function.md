# 05 · The function that sank Lagrange runs quantum physics

- **Length:** about 80 seconds.
- **Hook:** Here is a function whose every derivative at zero is zero, and
  quantum physics depends on it.

## Narration and scenes

| Time | Visual | On-screen text | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0–6 s | A flat curve hugging the axis near zero, then rising | e^(−1/x²) | Here is a function whose every derivative at zero is zero, and quantum physics depends on it. |
| 6–20 s | A card for Lagrange; a power series unrolls | f(x+i) = f(x) + p·i + q·i² + … | Around 1800 Lagrange tried to build calculus without limits. Every function is a power series, and the derivative is simply a coefficient. Pure algebra. He even boasted that his *Analytical Mechanics* contained no figures. |
| 20–36 s | Zoom on the flat function at zero; its series reads 0 + 0 + 0 + … | Every coefficient: 0 | Cauchy answered with this function: e to the minus one over x squared. At zero the function and all its derivatives vanish, so its power series is zero everywhere. The function is still positive for every x except zero. Algebra by coefficients cannot see it. |
| 36–52 s | A particle facing a barrier; a faint wave leaks through | e^(−A/ħ) | Now put Planck's constant where x squared was. e to the minus A over h-bar is how strongly a quantum particle tunnels through a barrier. Expand it in powers of h-bar and every term is zero. |
| 52–62 s | Quote card | "all terms are zero" | The physicist Michael Berry puts it plainly: such a wave cannot be expanded in a power series, all terms are zero. |

## Sources and status

- **VERIFIED** (mathematics) e^(−1/x²), extended by 0 at x = 0, has every
  derivative equal to zero there.
- **VERIFIED** Berry, "Asymptotics, singularities and the reduction of
  theories" (1994), p. 602: "Again this is singular, and cannot be expanded
  in a power series in λ (all terms are zero)." On p. 603 he makes ħ play
  the role of λ.
- **VERIFIED** (physics) Tunnelling amplitudes carry factors e^(−A/ħ) with A
  a Euclidean action; their expansion in ħ vanishes term by term.
- **MEMORY** Lagrange, *Théorie des fonctions analytiques* (1797): the
  derivative as the coefficient of i in f(x+i). Check the edition.
- **MEMORY** Lagrange, *Mécanique analytique* (1788), Avertissement: "On ne
  trouvera point de Figures dans cet Ouvrage." Check the wording.
- **MEMORY** Cauchy's use of e^(−1/x²) against Lagrange's foundation, around
  1823. Check the source and date before production.
- **MEMORY** P. A. M. Dirac, "The Lagrangian in Quantum Mechanics",
  *Physikalische Zeitschrift der Sowjetunion* 3 (1933). Check the reference.
- **INTERPRETATION** "Carries his name in its exponent": the action is the
  time integral of the Lagrangian, in its Euclidean form for tunnelling.

## Do not say

- That Lagrange's calculus was useless. His algebraic view survives in
  power-series methods; Cauchy's example shows where it stops.
- That tunnelling is impossible to compute. It is invisible to the
  power series in ħ and is computed by other means.
